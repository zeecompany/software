### 1 · THE 28-YEAR HIGH  *(BG: stormy terraced street)*
This week, something happened in Britain's financial markets that hasn't happened since 1998. And if you've got a mortgage, pay rent, or pay tax, it's about to land on your doorstep. On Tuesday, the UK's long-term borrowing costs spiked to a 28-year high. Within a day, the rates banks use to price your mortgage hit a three-year high. With 1.8 million fixed-rate deals ending this year, and a Budget on the 28th of October that could bring up to 25 billion pounds of tax rises, this is the moment to pay attention. In the next six minutes: what's happened, what it means for your monthly payments, and five things you can do about it right now.

### 2 · THE NUMBERS  *(BG: City of London skyline)*
Let's start with the numbers, because they're genuinely startling. On Tuesday the 1st of September, the yield on 30-year UK government bonds, known as gilts, hit 5.89 per cent. The highest since March 1998. The 10-year gilt climbed above 5.2 per cent, its highest since June 2008, the depths of the financial crisis. And Britain suffered the biggest jump of any G7 country. Here's why that matters to you. Lenders don't price fixed mortgages off the Bank of England base rate. They price them off swap rates. On Wednesday, the five-year swap rate rose above 4.52 per cent, a three-year high. When swaps move, mortgage rates follow. Sometimes within days.

### 3 · WHY IT'S HAPPENING  *(BG: oil tanker / refinery)*
So why is this happening? It's a chain reaction, and it starts thousands of miles away. This week, the United States and Iran exchanged fire for the first time in a month. Oil jumped nearly four per cent to almost 94 dollars a barrel. Higher oil means higher inflation, and UK inflation had already ticked up to 2.9 per cent in July, with the Bank of England expecting around 3.2 per cent by year end. When investors fear inflation, they sell bonds. When bond prices fall, yields rise. Add worries about government borrowing, plus tech giants issuing debt to fund AI data centres, and you get a global sell-off. Britain, already with the highest borrowing costs in the G7, got hit hardest.

### 4 · THE 1.8 MILLION CLIFF  *(BG: worried couple at kitchen table)*
Now, the part that hits millions of households directly. According to UK Finance, around 1.8 million fixed-rate mortgage deals end in 2026. Roughly half are five-year fixes taken out in 2021, when the average five-year rate was just 2.75 per cent, and many people locked in below two. Today, the average five-year fix is 5.63 per cent. The average two-year is 5.59. So even before this week's turmoil, hundreds of thousands of families were already facing a payment shock. And the next Bank of England decision is on the 17th of September. Markets expected rates to stay at 3.75 per cent all year, but three members of the committee already voted for a hike back in July.

### 5 · YOUR PAYMENT SHOCK  *(BG: keys, calculator, letters)*
Let's put real numbers on it. Take a typical 200,000-pound repayment mortgage over 25 years. Fixed in 2021 at 2.75 per cent, that costs around 923 pounds a month. Re-fix today at 5.63 per cent, and it jumps to roughly 1,244 pounds. That's 321 pounds more, every single month. Over 3,800 pounds a year. If you got a sub-two per cent deal, the jump is closer to 400 pounds a month. And that's before any further rises. As AJ Bell's Russ Mould put it this week: if bond yields rise, credit card, mortgage and car loan rates will rise too, because lenders protect their margins. This isn't a forecast. It's arithmetic.

### 6 · THE BUDGET TAX RAID  *(BG: despatch box, Westminster)*
And then there's the Budget. On the 28th of October, Chancellor John Healey delivers his first Budget, and this bond rout has just made it much harder. Economists say the sell-off could nearly halve his fiscal headroom, from 26 billion pounds to under 14. Capital Economics is warning of up to 25 billion pounds in tax rises. And the Resolution Foundation has told the Chancellor point-blank: if you want three per cent of GDP on defence, middle earners have to pay more. Labour has promised not to raise income tax, VAT or National Insurance rates. That leaves capital gains tax, pension tax relief, inheritance tax, a possible new defence or social care levy, and the quiet one: frozen thresholds, dragging millions into higher tax bands without a single headline rate changing.

### 7 · 5 THINGS TO DO NOW  *(BG: woman organising documents, warm light)*
So what can you actually do? Five things. One: find out exactly when your fixed deal ends. Not roughly. The date. Two: many lenders let you lock in a new rate up to six months early. Lock one in now. If rates fall before you complete, you can usually switch to the cheaper deal. Three: whatever you do, don't drift onto your lender's standard variable rate. That's where the real damage happens. Four: if you're still on a cheap fix, overpay while it's cheap. Most deals allow ten per cent a year penalty-free, and every pound you clear now is a pound you won't refinance at 5.6 per cent. Five: with the Budget eight weeks away, use this year's Isa and pension allowances while the rules are still the rules. This isn't financial advice, but it is what the numbers are telling you. If in doubt, speak to an independent adviser.

### 8 · YOUR TURN  *(BG: suburban sunrise aerial)*
Here's the uncomfortable truth. Whether it's a higher mortgage, higher rent, or a bigger tax bill, this week's bond rout is going to reach almost everyone in Britain, one way or another. So I want to hear from you. Is your fixed rate ending in the next twelve months? What rate are you coming off, and what are you being offered? Drop it in the comments, because real numbers help everyone watching. If this was useful, subscribe. I'll be covering the Bank of England decision on the 17th and the Budget on the 28th of October as they happen. Thanks for watching, and look after your money.
